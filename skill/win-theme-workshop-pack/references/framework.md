# Win theme framework

This is the reusable IP behind every win theme workshop pack and every win-theme-development write-up. It doesn't change between pursuits — only the content poured into it does. Both the `win-theme-workshop-pack` and `win-theme-development` skills build on this same framework so a theme sketched in the workshop and the theme written up afterwards use identical language and structure.

## The four-part formula

Every win theme is built by answering four separate questions, in order. Resist the urge to jump straight to a punchy statement — a theme that skips straight to "why us" without an established client priority behind it is marketing copy, not a win theme.

| # | Question | What good looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Client priority** — What must the client achieve or avoid? | Written in the client's own language, not ours. Traceable to a source signal. |
| 2 | **Why us** — What can we bring that matters? | Relevant to the priority *and* genuinely hard for another credible bidder to match — not just true of us. |
| 3 | **Proof** — How do we make it credible? | A specific evidence, example or demonstration (a reference, a metric, a named capability) — never an unsupported assertion. |
| 4 | **Quantifiable benefit** — What changes, by how much? | Has a baseline, a measure and a timeframe. "Better" and "faster" are not benefits until they have numbers attached. |

## The signal map

Before drafting themes, sort what's actually known about the client into two tiers so the room can see what's proven versus inferred:

- **Primary signals** — priorities stated explicitly, repeated across documents, or directly used as evaluation/scoring criteria. These carry the most weight and should anchor the strongest themes.
- **Secondary signals** — priorities implied by a requirement, constraint or risk rather than stated outright. Useful for shaping proof points and risk-mitigation messaging, but shouldn't be the sole basis for a headline theme until corroborated.

Every signal needs a citation — the document, clause, question number or page it came from. A signal without a citation is an opinion the team holds, not something the client has told them; mark it as such and either validate it or leave it out of the map.

## The seven-point final check

Run every drafted win theme statement through these seven questions before it leaves the room. The goal is to catch anything generic enough that a competitor could say the same thing.

1. **Why us** — "The client should choose us because…" (best fit, unique value, what they gain by selecting us)
2. **What makes us different** — "Unlike other bidders, we…" (people, experience, delivery approach, tools/accelerators, relationships, way of working)
3. **What worries the client** — "The client's biggest risk is… and we address it by…" (delivery risk, stakeholder engagement, timeframes, change management, technical complexity, governance cost)
4. **Strongest evidence** — "Our strongest proof point is…" (similar projects, measurable outcomes, testimonials, industry recognition, certifications/case studies, experienced personnel)
5. **Client outcome** — "By choosing us, the client will be able to…" (focus on outcomes, not activities: deliver sooner and reduce risk, improve services and build internal capability, achieve long-term sustainability)
6. **Finish the sentence** — "We don't just provide ___, we provide ___." (e.g., technology → confidence; advice → practical outcomes; project management → successful delivery; resources → trusted partners)
7. **Competitive gap** — "If we lose, what will the winning bidder have convinced the client of?" (use the answer to close our own gaps and sharpen the message)

### The believability test

Apply this last, to the whole statement: **if a competitor used this exact statement, would it still be believable?**

- **Yes** → it's too generic. Sharpen it until it's specific to this client and this bid.
- **No** → that's a real win theme. Keep it.
