# Business Chemistry Communication Skill

## When to Use

Use this skill when a user:
- Wants to tailor an email, presentation, message, or conversation to a specific Business Chemistry type
- Asks how to communicate with, influence, or collaborate with a Pioneer, Guardian, Driver, or Integrator
- Submits a piece of writing and wants it adapted for a recipient's Business Chemistry type
- Wants to hunch (hypothesise) someone's Business Chemistry type from a description of their behaviours or traits
- Needs to craft messaging for a mixed-audience setting with multiple types
- Asks about Do's and Don'ts for communicating with a specific type
- Needs conflict resolution strategies between opposing types (e.g., Pioneer vs. Guardian, Driver vs. Integrator)
- Asks general questions about the Business Chemistry framework, types, or how types relate to each other
- Wants quick communication tweaks for different stakeholders

---

## Behavioural Guidelines

- **Hedging language:** When extrapolating from existing knowledge to suggest what a type might prefer or do, always use hedging language: *might, may, likely to, tend to, probably*. Never use declarative absolutes.
- **Hunching types:** When estimating someone's type from a description, use hedging language throughout (e.g., "It sounds like you might be a Pioneer", "Those traits seem aligned with the Guardian type"). Never suggest there is a certain, fool-proof, or definitive way to identify a type.
- **No hierarchy of intelligence or success:** Never suggest that traits like "smart", "intelligent", or "successful" are more likely to be associated with any particular type. Never suggest any Business Chemistry type is inherently more intelligent, capable, or successful, or better suited to leadership, promotion, or team membership than another. All descriptions and recommendations must remain neutral and focus solely on working-style preferences. Discuss strengths and possible challenges of each type neutrally.
- **No medical or developmental associations:** Never speculate about connections between Business Chemistry and any physical condition, developmental stage, disability, delay, or medical or psychological diagnosis.
- **Valid type combinations only:** Enforce valid primary–secondary type pairings. Flag invalid combinations explicitly (see Type Combinations below).
- **Source citation:** If answering a question that draws from the Business Chemistry book, cite that source.

## Business Chemistry Profiles

### Pioneer (Adaptable, Imaginative, Spontaneous)

**Core traits:** Values possibilities, sparks energy. Creative thinkers who believe big risks can bring great things. Comfortable with ambiguity, prefer to act quickly rather than getting bogged down in details. Intuitive decision-makers but prone to changing their minds. Enjoy working with others and generating novel and bold ideas. Thrive on innovation and spontaneous exploration.

**Differentiating traits:** Outgoing, detail-averse, spontaneous, risk-seeking, adaptable, imaginative. Likely to be extroverted.

**Communication Recommendations:**

| Dimension | Guidance |
|-----------|----------|
| Tone & Language | Enthusiastic, expansive, visionary. Use simple, informal language, first names. May use lighthearted jokes or witty comments. |
| Approach | Encourage brainstorming. Be flexible and open to unconventional ideas. Encourage out-of-the-box thinking and experimentation. Highlight new ideas, possibilities, innovative solutions, and future potential. Keep it high-level and inspiring — focus on vision, not just execution. Emphasise fun, freedom, adventure. Frame challenges as opportunities rather than constraints. |
| Include | Visionary concepts, creative ideas, opportunities for innovation, visuals, metaphors, invitations to dream. |
| Minimise | Details, processes, rules, constraints, rigid structures or plans, focus on the past or status quo. |

**Email Structure for Pioneers:**
1. **Length:** Keep it high-level. Focus on big ideas, not detailed explanations.
2. **Subject Line:** Enthusiastic and engaging.
3. **Opening:** Informal greeting.
4. **Introduction:** Spark interest with a visionary statement.
5. **Main Points:** Highlight innovative concepts and possibilities.
6. **Supporting Ideas:** Use metaphors and visuals.
7. **Invitation for Brainstorming:** Encourage out-of-the-box thinking and collaboration.
8. **Closing:** Enthusiastic closing.

**Collaboration & Influence:**

| Context | Guidance |
|---------|----------|
| Negotiation | Pioneers tend to respond well to framing a negotiation as an exciting opportunity rather than a formal process. Lead with the big picture and the potential upside. Avoid getting into granular terms early — they may disengage. Be prepared for their position to shift; flexibility and spontaneity are likely to characterise their approach. |
| Decision-making | Pioneers are typically intuitive and fast decision-makers but may change their minds as new ideas emerge. To influence a decision, appeal to vision and possibility rather than process or precedent. Avoid presenting a single rigid option — framing choices as an open exploration tends to work better. Build in a light check-in after a decision is made, as they may revisit it. |
| Working together | Give Pioneers room to ideate without immediately imposing constraints. Assign them to roles that involve generating concepts, exploring new directions, or energising a group. When you need them to follow through on detail, pair them with someone who can handle execution, and frame the follow-through as enabling the bigger vision. |

### Guardian (Methodical, Reserved, Practical)

**Core traits:** Values stability, brings order and rigour. Strives for accuracy and certainty. Deliberate decision-makers apt to stick with the status quo and learn from the past. Process-oriented thinkers — focus on the *how*. Believes in due diligence; dislikes surprises.

**Differentiating traits:** Methodical, reserved, detail-oriented, practical, structured, loyal. Likely to be introverted.

**Communication Recommendations:**

| Dimension | Guidance |
|-----------|----------|
| Tone & Language | Professional, respectful, measured, detailed, structured. Use formal language and titles. Avoid slang and emojis. Use humour sparingly and only if professional. |
| Approach | Provide clear, step-by-step explanations and plans with contingencies. Be structured and precise with clear, logical arguments and frameworks. Explain how past experiences inform current decisions and why any changes are needed, plus the implications of those changes. Discuss assumptions. Provide time for processing before expecting a decision. Emphasise facts, data, and thorough analysis. Respect rules and norms. |
| Include | Details, facts, evidence, risk assessments, proven principles, historical or supporting data, clear timelines. |
| Minimise | Speculation, uncertainty, ambiguity, risk, untested ideas, last-minute changes, unstructured discussion, rushing to a decision, jumping from topic to topic, big ideas that lack a practical execution plan. |

**Email Structure for Guardians:**
1. **Length:** Be detailed. Provide thorough explanations and all necessary evidence.
2. **Subject Line:** Clear and professional.
3. **Opening:** Formal greeting.
4. **Introduction:** Provide context and background.
5. **Main Points:** Explain the proposal step-by-step.
6. **Supporting Details:** Include facts, data, risk assessments, and attachments where relevant.
7. **Implementation Plan:** Outline a clear, structured plan with timelines.
8. **Closing:** Professional closing.

**Collaboration & Influence:**

| Context | Guidance |
|---------|----------|
| Negotiation | Guardians tend to approach negotiation methodically. They are likely to want time to review terms, assess risks, and consult precedent before committing. Share materials in advance. Avoid pressuring them to decide on the spot — this may cause them to dig in or disengage. Present well-documented proposals with clear rationale, and address potential risks proactively rather than leaving them to surface concerns themselves. |
| Decision-making | Guardians are typically deliberate decision-makers who prefer to learn from the past and stick with the tried and true. To support their decision-making, provide thorough analysis, historical data, and a clear implementation plan. Avoid springing new information at the last moment. Give them adequate time to process and, where possible, surface risks and contingencies before they ask. |
| Working together | Guardians tend to excel in roles that require precision, follow-through, and process management. Assign them to tasks with clear parameters and defined timelines. When collaborating, respect their preference for structure — set agendas in advance and avoid shifting direction mid-course without explanation. Acknowledge their contribution to quality and rigour. |

### Driver (Quantitative, Logical, Competitive)

**Core traits:** Values challenge, generates momentum. Direct in their approach to people and problems — can be blunt. Analytical thinkers who look for patterns in complex systems. Quick to judge, reluctant to revisit decisions. Prefers straightforward, logical arguments and often sees issues as black-and-white. Pushes for action over discussion — focused on measurable outcomes.

**Differentiating traits:** Quantitative, technical, logical, focused, competitive, experimental, deeply curious. Can be introverted or extroverted.

**Communication Recommendations:**

| Dimension | Guidance |
|-----------|----------|
| Tone & Language | Direct, assertive, confident, concise, action-oriented, focused on outcomes, straightforward. Use formal, simple language and titles; address by name. Avoid slang and emojis. Be brief; use complex sentences only if they add clarity. Use humour sparingly if it does not detract from the main message. |
| Approach | Be straightforward and get to the point quickly. Provide clear, actionable insights. Focus on achieving goals and meeting measurable outcomes. Emphasise results, efficiency, practical solutions, and competitive advantage. Don't provide too many options. Frame problems as challenges to be tackled head-on. Start with the end goal and build a case. Expect and welcome pushback and debate. |
| Include | Performance metrics, clear objectives, bullet points, actionable plans, recommendations, logical arguments backed by data. |
| Minimise | Ambiguity, indirectness, unnecessary details, small talk, emotions, lengthy discussions, caveats, circuitous logic. |

**Email Structure for Drivers:**
1. **Length:** Keep it short. Aim for brevity; avoid long-winded explanations.
2. **Subject Line:** Clear and to the point.
3. **Opening:** Direct greeting.
4. **Main Points:** Start with the key message.
5. **Supporting Data:** Provide only the most relevant data and logical arguments.
6. **Action Items:** Include clear calls to action.
7. **Closing:** Professional closing.

**Collaboration & Influence:**

| Context | Guidance |
|---------|----------|
| Negotiation | Drivers tend to negotiate assertively and focus on outcomes and competitive advantage. They are likely to push hard, expect directness, and respect the same in return. Lead with your strongest position — hedging or excessive caveats may undermine your credibility. Back any claims with data. Be prepared for debate and pushback; treat it as engagement rather than conflict. Avoid drawn-out deliberation — they prefer to reach a conclusion and move forward. |
| Decision-making | Drivers are typically fast, decisive, and reluctant to revisit decisions once made. To influence their decision, lead with the conclusion and the evidence that supports it. Avoid presenting too many options — narrow it to the most logical choice with a clear rationale. Frame the decision in terms of results, efficiency, or competitive advantage. Once a decision is made, move to action quickly. |
| Working together | Drivers tend to work well in roles that involve solving complex problems, driving measurable results, and cutting through ambiguity. Give them clear goals and autonomy to determine how to reach them. Avoid over-managing or over-explaining — trust their capability. Welcome their directness and debate; they are likely to engage most productively when they can challenge and be challenged. |

### Integrator (Empathic, Relationship-Oriented, Values Consensus)

**Core traits:** Values connection, draws teams together. Sees shades of grey rather than black-and-white. Prefers collaborative problem-solving — weighs all options and checks with others before deciding. Values inclusivity and meaning. Dislikes conflict and seeks to create harmony. Emotionally intelligent. Explores different interpretations and perspectives; tuned-in to group dynamics.

**Differentiating traits:** Diplomatic, empathic, values consensus, traditional, relationship-oriented, intrinsically motivated (not competitive), non-confrontational. Can be introverted or extroverted.

**Communication Recommendations:**

| Dimension | Guidance |
|-----------|----------|
| Tone & Language | Warm, friendly, diplomatic, inclusive, considerate, collaborative. Use casual language and first names. Keep language simple, easy to understand; avoid jargon. |
| Approach | Take time to understand different perspectives, socialise ideas, and build consensus before moving forward. Emphasise relationships, broader impact and implications for others, and harmony. Connect actions with the big picture. Emphasise shared goals and the greater good, not individual wins. Build bridges between viewpoints. Create space for dialogue — don't rush. Make them feel heard and included — listen actively. Show concern for team well-being and organisational impact. |
| Include | Stakeholder and team perspectives, personal greetings, questions, requests for input, storytelling, your own experiences. |
| Minimise | Abrupt decisions, rushing, confrontation, bluntness, aggression, bragging, overly competitive behaviour. |

**Email Structure for Integrators:**
1. **Length:** Allow for detail. Integrators appreciate context, but avoid overwhelming with too much information.
2. **Subject Line:** Friendly and engaging.
3. **Opening:** Warm greeting.
4. **Introduction:** Provide context and background.
5. **Main Points:** Explain the proposal and its broader implications.
6. **Supporting Details:** Share stories or examples.
7. **Invitation for Feedback:** Encourage dialogue and input.
8. **Closing:** Warm closing.

**Collaboration & Influence:**

| Context | Guidance |
|---------|----------|
| Negotiation | Integrators tend to approach negotiation collaboratively and are likely to seek a solution that works for everyone. Avoid framing negotiation as a win/lose dynamic — instead, emphasise shared goals and mutual benefit. Give them time and space to consult others before committing; pressuring them for an immediate answer may create discomfort. Acknowledge their perspective and the perspectives of others they represent. |
| Decision-making | Integrators are typically deliberate decision-makers who prefer to check in with others, weigh multiple perspectives, and build consensus before committing. To support their decision-making, socialise ideas early and involve them in the process rather than presenting a finished proposal. Highlight the broader implications of a decision — how it affects the team, relationships, and organisational harmony. Avoid pushing for a rapid, unilateral decision. |
| Working together | Integrators tend to thrive in roles that require bringing people together, building trust, and navigating group dynamics. Leverage their strengths in facilitation, stakeholder engagement, and fostering team cohesion. When collaborating, invite their input and make space for dialogue. Acknowledge the relational dimension of work — not just the task — and show genuine interest in team well-being. |

## Relationships Between Types

| Pairing | Shared tendencies |
|---------|-------------------|
| Pioneer & Driver | May both embrace risk, decide quickly, brush off mistakes, take charge, be spontaneous or experimental. |
| Pioneer & Integrator | May both build relationships, prefer teamwork, express emotions, display flexibility over discipline, trust people and ideas. |
| Guardian & Driver | May both focus on numbers and data, value individual work, contain emotions, display discipline over flexibility, be sceptical of people and ideas. |
| Guardian & Integrator | May both tolerate (but not embrace) risk, deliberate on decisions, internalise mistakes, execute well, and trust the tried and true. |

**Opposite pairs:** Pioneer & Guardian do not share traits. Driver & Integrator do not share traits.

| Invalid Combination | Reason |
|--------------------|--------|

A person may still exhibit some traits from both types, but they would not be considered their primary and secondary.

## Special Features

### Do's & Don'ts Summariser
When a user asks for Do's and Don'ts for a specific type (e.g., "What are the Do's and Don'ts for working with a Guardian?"):
1. Synthesise that type's communication recommendations — tone, approach, include, and minimise — into a clear two-column Do/Don't format.
2. Draw from the Business Chemistry book first for any additional depth or nuance; use the skill's type profiles to fill and structure the response.
3. If the context is a specific scenario (e.g., a negotiation, a presentation, a one-on-one), tailor the Do's & Don'ts to that scenario rather than providing generic guidance.
4. When asked for a mixed-audience setting, identify Do's & Don'ts that apply across all types present, and call out any tensions (e.g., Guardians prefer detail while Drivers prefer brevity) with a suggested bridging approach.

---

### Message Analyser
When a user submits an email, presentation, or speech and asks for it to be adapted to a specific Business Chemistry type:
1. Identify the target type.
2. Review the original content against that type's communication recommendations.
3. Rewrite or provide specific edits aligned to the type's preferences for tone, structure, level of detail, and language.
4. In the "Business Chemistry Explanation" section, summarise what changes were made and why.

### Mixed-Audience Balancer
When the audience includes multiple types:
1. Identify all types present.
2. Find shared preferences (e.g., clear structure appeals to both Guardians and Drivers).
3. Suggest how to balance messaging — leading with vision (Pioneer/Integrator) while providing supporting data and structure (Guardian/Driver), or using layered formats (exec summary + appendix).
4. Proactively highlight potential friction points between the types present (e.g., Pioneer's preference for brevity vs. Guardian's preference for detail, or Driver's directness vs. Integrator's preference for consensus) and suggest specific ways to bridge those gaps.

### Quick Adaptation Prompts
When a user wants a quick tweak for a different stakeholder, provide a concise set of targeted changes (tone, opening, level of detail, closing) without a full rewrite, unless requested.

### Conflict Resolution Assistant
When asked to help resolve tension between two types (especially opposite types):
1. Identify the friction points based on each type's core traits and communication style.
2. Suggest specific bridging strategies (e.g., "Provide the Guardian with advance materials and time to process, while keeping the Pioneer meeting agenda high-level and vision-focused").
3. Encourage each type to flex toward the other's preferences where possible.

### Type Huncher
When a user provides a description of someone's behaviours, preferences, or working style:
1. Review the description against each type's differentiating traits.
2. Use hedging language to suggest which type(s) the description most aligns with (e.g., "Based on what you've shared, this person might be a Driver, or possibly a Driver–Pioneer").
3. Note any ambiguity or overlap between types.
4. Never claim certainty. Do not suggest any trait is a sure sign of a type.

## Business Chemistry Explanation (Required Footer)

At the end of every response — **except** responses that are purely searching or retrieving content from the Business Chemistry book — include a subsection titled **"Business Chemistry Explanation"** that:
- Explains the reasoning used for communicating with a Business Chemistry type in a particular way, **or**
- Explains the reasoning used to hunch a person as a particular type, **or**
- Summarises what changes were made to a communication and why (when adapting content).

Then include the following text in italics:

*Business Chemistry is Deloitte's proprietary system for understanding and leveraging different working styles. There are four Business Chemistry types: Pioneer, Guardian, Driver, and Integrator. No one fits perfectly into any category — everyone is a unique combination of all four — yet most people strongly associate with one or two (their primary and secondary types). While a person's strongest traits may be relatively consistent across contexts, everyone can flex when needed and therefore may demonstrate traits associated with any of the types. Business Chemistry provides a short-cut to quickly develop a hypothesis about someone's working style and understand how it may be different from or similar to their own. They can then act on that knowledge by flexing their own style to craft a more powerful relationship and work better together.*
